One word at a time — is it faster?

A field comparison of RSVP presentation — one word at a fixed point on screen — with ordinary self-paced PDF reading.

The hypothesis is not supported by this dataset.

PDF was faster for three of four participants. Confidence is low: the sample is tiny, the protocol was not randomized, and comprehension was self-rated.

01 / Method

A controlled idea, tested on a very small sample

Each participant read four Russian-language texts split into two parts. One half appeared in RSVP mode; the other opened as an ordinary PDF. Format order alternated across adjacent texts.

For RSVP, participants first selected a pace from 50 to 700 words per minute. Words then appeared automatically one at a time, with pauses included in observed time. PDF reading was self-paced until the participant pressed the completion button. After each segment, participants rated comprehension from 1 to 5.

How speed was calculated

The exact word count for each text half was recovered using the same tokenizer as the application. Effective speed was calculated as words / observed time × 60. To avoid treating 16 segments from one person as independent observations, words and time were first summed within each participant and format.

The partIndex field was absent from 16 early segments. It was deterministically recovered as orderInText, following the protocol contract. Names, email addresses, name-derived source filenames, and full session identifiers are not published.

Data-quality check

Two PDF segments for P2 exceeded 1,000 words/min. That pattern is more consistent with scanning or an early completion click than careful reading. The observation remains in the primary analysis because no exclusion threshold was specified in advance. The median is more robust than the mean, but uncertainty is still substantial.

03 / Comprehension

Self-rated comprehension also leans toward PDF

Free-text feedback repeatedly mentioned lost context, an inability to linger on difficult words, and tokenization errors. The only participant who read faster with RSVP explicitly said they enjoy speed reading.

04 / Conclusion

The hypothesis is not supported by this dataset

The collected data do not show that one-word presentation is faster than ordinary text. On the contrary, PDF was faster in three of four eligible sessions, and those same three participants gave PDF a higher comprehension rating.

Four people are not a basis for a universal conclusion. The strongest pilot signal is a possible interaction between format, selected pace, and prior comfort with speed reading. RSVP worked for the participant who selected 420 words/min and proved difficult for several slower participants.

The useful pilot takeaway is not “PDF is 1.81× faster.” It is this: a fixed word stream may accelerate a prepared reader, but it imposes costs when the reader needs to vary pace, recover context, or work around tokenization errors.

Primary limitations

  • A convenience sample of only four participants.
  • Format, text half, and order were not randomized.
  • RSVP was calibration-paced; PDF was self-paced.
  • Pauses mix perceptual speed with interface usability.
  • Comprehension was self-rated, not objectively tested.
  • Two PDF segments showed extreme speeds.

A stronger next experiment

  1. Randomize each text half’s format and counterbalance order.
  2. Use unfamiliar texts matched for length and difficulty.
  3. Record active reading time separately from pauses.
  4. Ask 3–5 objective comprehension questions per segment.
  5. Predefine exclusions and the primary outcome.
  6. Recruit at least several dozen participants.

Context from the research literature

RSVP research has produced mixed results. Early laboratory work reported high reading speeds without saccades, and small-screen experiments sometimes found a speed advantage without a clear comprehension penalty. More recent experiments and reviews emphasize the speed–accuracy trade-off, the loss of regressions, and poorer performance on some comprehension measures at high presentation rates.
